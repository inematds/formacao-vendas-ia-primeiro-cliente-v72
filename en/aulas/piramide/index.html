Lesson 1 · 16 min

What loses value and what stays

Selling AI is not one single thing. It comes in layers: some become more valuable over time, others turn into a commodity and the price collapses. Building a website, a store or a bot has become easy, and anyone who sells only that ends up competing on price. This lesson separates the layers and builds your portfolio on the base that lasts.

Start the lesson

In this lesson you will

  1. 1Read the AI money pyramid: high ticket × useful life
  2. 2Choose the right way in when you are starting from zero
  3. 3Look at margin, not revenue
  4. 4Build a portfolio of 4 offers where building is part of a service

Goal: separate the build offers, which lose value, from the trust offers, which accumulate, and put together your portfolio of 4 offers.

01 · The AI money pyramid

From top to base: the ticket goes down, the useful life goes up. Click on each layer.

In Professional 2030 the base has a name: diagnose, review, follow up and teach.

Your identity stays at the base: "I help companies trust what AI does," not "I build voice agents."

02 · The four ways in

Four reasons a company pays someone to run AI. They are not equally good.

1. Lack of knowledge

The client pays a lot because they don't know how simple it is.

It disappears as soon as the topic becomes a popular tutorial. Use it as cash flow, never as a plan.

2. Free sample

You teach or diagnose something small for free, the person sees real value and accepts a trial project.

It is the most reliable route for someone starting from zero.

Starting from zeroA well-done free diagnosis opens a door that no email ever will.

3. Unfair advantage

Did you work for years in logistics, accounting or insurance? Sell there.

You already speak the language, know the rules and know where it hurts.

4. Appearance pressure

Large companies get asked internally: "what are we doing about AI?"

That's where training and consulting reach the highest fees, but a beginner rarely gets there in the first year.

03 · Revenue is not profit

The number that shows up in screenshots is always revenue. On its own, it tells you nothing.

1. Margin per project

One big contract that pays a team can leave almost nothing. A smaller project done by you can leave much more.

A healthy benchmark for an operation with subcontractors: net margin between 35% and 45%.

2. Real hours

Count the hours spent on proposals and meetings that didn't close.

A project that's approved but not paid is a loss with a deadline.

3. Pieces that pull each other

The audit uncovers the problem; fixing that problem is the next sale.

People who have learned to use it ask for training; people who have something running ask for ongoing support.

CarefulMonthly advisory usually lasts two to three months. The antidote is in lesson 8: show the month 5 map in month 2.

4. The mistakes that sink you

Over-engineering, building on rotten data, promising a deadline that depends on third parties, picking a niche without data, and charging while apologizing.

Speak more simply than seems necessary.

04 · Templates to copy

The one-line calculation before you accept any project, and the portfolio prompt.

The one-line calculation
PROJETO: <nome>
Preço: <valor>  ·  Horas estimadas: <h>  ·  Custo de terceiros: <valor>
Margem = (preço − custo de terceiros − horas × <sua hora>) ÷ preço = <%>

Se a margem não fecha em pelo menos um terço:
[ ] o preço sobe   [ ] o escopo encolhe   [ ] eu recuso
Prompt · 90-day portfolio
You are my offer strategist. I'm going to build an AI services business
for the next 90 days, selling trust: diagnosing,
reviewing, supporting and teaching. Building only comes in as part of one
of those services.

My context:
- Industry where I already have real experience: <setor, ou "nenhum">
- What I already know how to build today: <liste, mesmo que pouco>
- Hours per week available: <número>
- How much I need to bill per month for this to be worth it: <valor>

Build for me:
1. FOUR offers, from the easiest to sell to the hardest. For each
   one: name, what's included, what's left out, delivery time,
   price range and why a client would buy THIS one.
2. The SEQUENCE: which offer opens the door to which, and the sentence I use
   to propose the next one after delivering the previous one.
3. The MATH: how many sales of each offer I need per month, with the hours
   I have. If it doesn't add up, say so plainly and propose what to cut.
4. Mark which offers will probably be commoditized in 12 months.

Don't invent demand. If information is missing, ask before guessing.

05 · Practice

Save your answers in a file with today's date. Read them again in 90 days.

1 · Your portfolio of 4 offers

30 min

Run the portfolio prompt with your context. Rewrite the result until every offer is a diagnosis, review, ongoing support or teaching; if there is building, it shows up inside one of those services.

Done when: the file has 4 offers with what's left out of each one, the sequence between them and the math closed in numbers; no offer is just "I build."

2 · The one-line calculation

10 min

Take your entry offer and fill in the one-line calculation with price, hours and third-party cost.

Done when: the margin is calculated in % and, if it came out below one third, you marked what changes: price, scope or decline.
